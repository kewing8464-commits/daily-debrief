For this project I built Daily Debrief, a nightly journal app where an AI interviews you about your day and turns the conversation into data like sleep, food, workouts, drinks and mood. Over time it shows which habits line up with your best and worst days.

My main AI tool was Claude Code, an AI coding agent. I acted as the product owner and it acted as the developer. Before any code was written it asked me questions, showed clickable design mockups in the browser, and wrote a design spec and step-by-step build plans that I approved. It wrote each feature test-first and before each release a separate AI reviewer read the code. Inside the app, a Groq-hosted language model runs the nightly interview and Whisper turns speech into text. I also built a connector so Claude or ChatGPT can answer questions about the journal using the app's own calculations.

The hardest challenge was making the insights honest. "Your recipe for a good day" ranks factors by how far a day's score sits above or below your average. The first version looked impressive but had real problems that the reviewer caught. It listed "also true" facts that were true by definition, counted "Jake" and "jake" as two people, and called the last seven entries a "7-day average" even when they covered two weeks.

To fix each problem I had the AI write a test that reproduced it and failed, then change the code until the test passed. I also added rules: a factor needs at least four days with it and four without, every comparison shows its day count, and every result carries the line "patterns, not proof of cause." Later a debrief crashed on my phone. The server logs showed no error, and the timing matched a new deploy, so we traced it to an old browser tab loading code that no longer existed. Now the app reloads itself once and reports any browser error.

The AI was fast, careful about testing, and good at catching its own bugs in review. It struggled with things outside the code: a placeholder setting it left behind broke AI summaries, and its first color palette failed a colorblind check until it ran a validator. It also cannot decide what matters to a person.

That part took human judgment. I decided what to track, what counts as a good day, and that the wording should be honest instead of impressive. I rejected a body-fat estimate because weight, food and workouts cannot reliably separate fat from muscle. I also set the privacy rules: each account sees only its own data, and the AI connector is read-only.

I trust the app for personal reflection, but not yet for real business decisions. That would take more data per pattern, statistical significance tests, a person confirming every AI summary before it is saved (already required), ongoing error monitoring, and treating every pattern as a hypothesis to test rather than a cause.
